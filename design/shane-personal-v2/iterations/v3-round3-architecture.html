The variable is architecture again, but the visual system is settled: the Drafting apparatus is now the constant across all five — dimensioned figures with witness marks, 45° hatch for the region under discussion, and numbered callouts keyed to notes in the margin. Architecture A

Drawing First

The drawing opens the page at full width and the prose below it is the key — three numbered blocks, each one answering a callout on the drawing above. It inverts the usual order: you're shown the shape of the idea before you're told about it, and the writing exists to explain a figure rather than the figure existing to decorate the writing.

This is the architecture that takes the Drafting apparatus most literally. It only works if a page can be reduced to one drawing — which is a useful editorial constraint, and a real limitation.

Reading order
Figure → key → supporting text → index. The headline is demoted below the drawing and set at section scale, not hero scale.
Where nav lives
A single top rule with the wordmark and four links. No rail — the key columns occupy the horizontal space a rail would take.
Holds up on
Article pages (one drawing, then the argument), the about page, any of the field guides.
Breaks on
Anything with no drawing. A post that's pure prose has nothing to lead with, and the architecture has no fallback that doesn't look broken.

Architecture B

Facing Spread

Two columns of equal weight with a hairline gutter between them: the argument runs down the left, the apparatus runs down the right, and horizontal registration lines cross both so a figure always sits level with the paragraph that references it. It reads like an open technical manual where the plate faces the text.

Unlike a margin rail, the right column is not subordinate. It carries drawings at full size, code, and callout keys — content, not chrome.

Reading order
Band by band. Each registration band is a complete unit: claim on the left, evidence on the right, both terminated by the same rule.
Where nav lives
Top rule only. The right column is too valuable to spend on links.
Holds up on
Long-form technical writing, which is 21 of your 32 posts. The band structure is a genuine drafting tool — it forces every claim to face its evidence.
Breaks on
Uneven bands. If the left runs long and the right has nothing to say, you get a half-empty spread, and there's no honest way to pad it.

Architecture C

Sheet

The page stops being a document and becomes a drawing sheet: a hard border, zone references lettered down the left edge and numbered across the top, content sitting in the field, and a title block in the lower right carrying sheet number, revision, date, and author. Every page is a sheet in a set.

The title block is the strongest idea here. It replaces the footer entirely and it's honest metadata — revision and date on a piece of writing is more useful than a social row.

Reading order
Field first, title block last — same as reading a real drawing. The sheet number is how you cite and link a page.
Where nav lives
Zone references double as an addressing scheme; the title block carries the sheet index link. There's no nav bar at all.
Holds up on
Everything, unusually. A sheet is content-agnostic — the field takes whatever you put in it, and the border and title block stay identical.
Breaks on
Scroll length. A real sheet has fixed proportions and a web page doesn't, so a long article leaves the title block stranded a very long way from the border it belongs to.

Architecture D

Assembly Order

The page is a numbered assembly running down a central axis: each step is a node on the axis with its text on one side and its detail drawing on the other, alternating. Long-form stops being an essay and becomes a sequence you can enter at step four.

This is the architecture your field guides already want. Eleven gates is an assembly, not a narrative, and forcing it into paragraphs is why it reads long.

Reading order
Down the axis, one node at a time. Steps are addressable — step 4 is a link, and the reader can start there without losing the thread.
Where nav lives
Top rule, plus the axis itself acts as a progress indicator on long pages.
Holds up on
The field guides, any process writing, the practice page (build → AEO → management is an assembly). Also the strongest of the five on mobile: the axis just slides left and everything stacks.
Breaks on
Non-sequential content. A page with three unordered ideas gets numbered anyway, which asserts an order that isn't real.

Run it by hand first

Concierge the whole workflow yourself before you automate a step of it. You're not testing whether it can be automated — you're finding out which part was actually the work.

Specify the failure, not the feature

"If this breaks next month, what turns red?" A spec that can't name its own alarm hasn't specified anything — it's described a demo, and demos don't survive contact with next month.

2

Let rigor scale to blast radius

Two gates for a copy change, all ten for anything touching billing or customer data. Process weight is a function of what you can't take back, not of how important the work feels.

Kill the framing before the tech

The tell is "you can only charge for it once." When a product only works as a one-time discovery, the engine is usually fine — it's pointed at the wrong job.

Architecture E

Set

The site is a drawing set and the sheet list never leaves the screen. A permanent left column lists every piece by number and title with the open one inverted in place; the rest of the width is the open sheet. Navigation is always visible and always shows where you are in the whole.

It's the most application-like of the five, and the only one where the size of the body of work is legible at all times — which for 32 pieces is an asset rather than a liability.

Reading order
List → open sheet. The list is the index page; there is no separate archive route, because you're always looking at the archive.
Where nav lives
It is the layout. Sections are groups in the list rather than links in a bar.
Holds up on
Browsing. Reading one piece then another costs no navigation, which is the single most common thing a returning visitor does.
Breaks on
Focus and mobile. A permanent list is a permanent distraction from a long read, and below 900px it collapses to a stacked block — at which point it's a normal page with a long list on top.

The Split Audience

The first reader of your docs isn't a developer. Every page now has two readers who want opposite things 1 — and the reflex, picking one, loses both 2.

1. Write for the model alone and you get answer-shaped mush no person finishes.

2. Write purely for the person and you're invisible to the thing that now decides what gets surfaced.

The way through is structural: state the answer flat, then earn it back. Both readers get served by the same paragraph in a different order.
